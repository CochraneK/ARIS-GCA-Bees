## Adjudication principles

The score anchors are applied literally:

- 0 = little credible evidence the mechanism materially shaped disciplinary formation;
- 1 = documented but peripheral/localized;
- 2 = substantial recurring role across important traditions/institutions;
- 3 = foundational/major role in institutional formation, methods, data infrastructure or professional expansion.

A colonial government merely **using** a discipline is not sufficient for 2 or 3. The mechanism must materially shape the academic/professional field. The frozen dimension boundaries are enforced: for example, removing antiquities is not treated as D5 resource extraction because D5 was defined around minerals, geological/resource inventories, economic botany and commercial extraction; antiquities collections belong under D9.

## Decisions

### D02 Archaeology — D1 Colonial administrative demand → **2**

Colonial archaeological services were recurring institutional homes for archaeology: the Archaeological Survey of India dates to 1861, and comparable antiquities departments operated in Egypt, Mandatory Palestine and other colonial settings. Recent review work on South Asia describes institutions, chronological frameworks and field methods as emerging from British colonial rule. This is stronger than localized use, but archaeology also had major noncolonial national/classical formation paths in Europe, so the evidence does not justify a discipline-wide 3.

Key evidence:
- Chakrabarti, *Archaeology and Coloniality in South Asia*, **Annual Review of Anthropology** 54 (2025), DOI 10.1146/annurev-anthro-072623-030252.
- Archaeological Survey of India institutional history (established 1861).
- Trigger, *A History of Archaeological Thought*, 2nd ed. (2006).
- Díaz-Andreu, *A World History of Nineteenth-Century Archaeology* (2007).

### D02 Archaeology — D5 Extraction/resource survey → **0**

Coder B's high score relied primarily on division/removal of finds. That is historically important, but it does not satisfy the frozen D5 construct. D5 concerns natural-resource survey/extraction infrastructures; archaeological study of mines or colonial extractive economies is the **object of archaeology**, not evidence that commercial resource extraction formed archaeology as a discipline. Antiquities removal is already captured by D9.

Targeted review found no credible formation-level channel linking colonial agricultural/forestry/ecological-transfer infrastructures to archaeology as an academic discipline. The existence of archaeological research on agriculture or landscape history does not satisfy D7.

### D03 Geography — D6 Colonial medicine/health governance → **1**

Medical geography has genuine imperial connections. Brown & Moon trace Jacques May from colonial surgery/tropical medicine into the development of medical geography; later histories describe disease ecology as having roots in colonial and tropical medicine, and disease mapping in China functioned within colonial/public-health projects. But this evidence concerns a recognizable **subfield**, not geography's discipline-wide formation, so 1 fits the frozen anchor better than 2.

Key evidence:
- Brown & Moon, “From Siam to New York: Jacques May and the ‘foundation’ of medical geography,” *Journal of Historical Geography* 30(4), 2004, DOI 10.1016/j.jhg.2003.08.018.
- Hanson, “Visualizing the Geography of the Diseases of China,” *Science in Context* 30(3), 2017, DOI 10.1017/S0269889717000205.
- Emch & Goel, “Disease ecology in health and medical geography,” *Singapore Journal of Tropical Geography*, 2025, DOI 10.1111/sjtg.12581.

### D08 Geology/Earth-resource sciences — D7 Agriculture/forestry/ecological transfer → **1**

There is real evidence for a colonial agrogeology/soil-science channel. Work on the Dutch East Indies documents government soil mapping tied to agricultural development and colonial population/land governance, while histories of tropical soil science trace colonial agricultural stations and survey training. This remains a specialized soil/agrogeology track rather than a major formative mechanism for geology as a whole.

Key evidence:
- Meijer, “Agrogeology’s Terrestrial Orders,” *Global Studies Quarterly* 6(2), 2026, DOI 10.1093/isagsq/ksag032.
- Tomking, *Soil science and the politics of expertise: Empire, land and fundamental research*, University of York thesis, 2025/26.
- Historical work on tropical soil survey and colonial agricultural science.

### D12 Economics — D9 Museums/archives/collections/specimens → **1**

Colonial archives and standardized Blue Books supply important long-run economic and fiscal evidence and are heavily used in colonial economic history. Gardner, for example, explicitly draws quantitative colonial public-finance evidence from annual Blue Books. This is meaningful source infrastructure, but it is largely an economic-history/empirical source channel rather than a discipline-building archive/collection system for economics as a whole.

Key evidence:
- Gardner, *Taxing Colonial Africa* (Oxford UP, 2012), source note on Colonial Blue Books and financial reports.
- UK National Archives / Cambridge RCS documentation of Colonial Blue Books, including standardized trade, revenue, population and expenditure statistics.

### D13 Public Administration/Social Policy — D3 Population/people/language classification → **2**

Colonial governance repeatedly depended on enumeration and categorical classification. British colonial census projects were explicitly pursued as oversight technologies; racial/caste/social categories were made administrative instruments and often persisted into postcolonial census systems. Because classification was a recurring apparatus of colonial administration across major imperial settings, this clears the “substantial recurring” threshold. It does not justify 3 because the core academic theories of public administration/social policy also have strong metropolitan lineages.

Key evidence:
- Christopher, “The quest for a census of the British Empire c.1840–1940,” *Journal of Historical Geography* 34(2), 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.jhg.2007.06.002.
- “Census, Caste Enumeration and the British Legacy,” *Oxford Handbook of Caste* (2024).
- Christopher, “Race and the census in the Commonwealth,” *Population, Space and Place* 11(2), 2005.

### D15 History — D3 Population/people/language classification → **2**

Colonial census, caste, race, tribe and ethnographic categories became durable historical source categories and shaped historiography of colonized societies. Dirks and related scholarship show how colonial classifications became enduring analytical frames. But the professional discipline of history also formed through national archives, Rankean seminar traditions and metropolitan university systems that were not dependent on colonial classification. Thus 2 rather than 3.

Key evidence:
- Dirks, *Castes of Mind: Colonialism and the Making of Modern India* (Princeton UP, 2001).
- Walby & Haan, “Caste Confusion and Census Enumeration in Colonial India, 1871–1921,” *Histoire sociale/Social History* 45(90), 2012, DOI 10.1353/his.2012.0026.
- Stoler, “Colonial Archives and the Arts of Governance,” *Archival Science* 2, 2002, DOI 10.1007/BF02435632.

### D16 Demography/Population Studies — D4 Overseas field sites/expeditions → **2**

This dimension is best read as repeated overseas field data collection rather than literal exploration expeditions. Gendreau documents roughly one hundred national colonial demographic operations across 46 sub-Saharan territories, including censuses and sample surveys, with most scientific operations in the late colonial period. Ittmann describes demography as policy science within the British Empire and proposals for on-site colonial demographic expertise. That is a recurring and substantial field-data infrastructure.

Key evidence:
- Gendreau, “Les opérations statistiques de collecte de données démographiques dans l'Afrique noire coloniale,” *Annales de Démographie Historique* (1987/1988), DOI 10.3406/adh.1988.1680.
- Ittmann, “Demography as Policy Science in the British Empire, 1918–1969,” *Journal of Policy History* 15(4), 2003, DOI 10.1353/jph.2003.0024.

### D17 Mathematics — D1 Colonial administrative demand → **1**

Imperial surveying and revenue administration generated large geodetic programs and mathematical work on measurement, adjustment and error. Ohnesorge shows physical geodesy stimulating precision instrumentation and statistics, and histories of British India show surveys were pursued for military/revenue purposes. However, these channels were peripheral to the major disciplinary formation of algebra, analysis, geometry and logic. The correct anchor is documented-but-peripheral.

Key evidence:
- Ohnesorge, “The promises and pitfalls of precision,” *Annals of Science* 81, 2024, DOI 10.1080/00033790.2023.2284335.
- Edney, *Mapping an Empire* (U. Chicago Press, 1997).
- Kumar, *Science and the Raj*, 2nd ed. (Oxford UP, 2006), chapter on survey institutions.

### D18 Physics — D1 Colonial administrative demand → **1**

British government and imperial networks funded magnetic surveys and colonial observatories for navigation and global measurement. Gillin describes a government-financed worldwide magnetic enterprise using naval expeditions and colonial observatories, and Naylor documents meteorology at colonial physical observatories. These were important imperial physical-science infrastructures but sit largely in geophysics/meteorology and do not amount to a substantial formative mechanism for the later core of physics.

Key evidence:
- Gillin, *An Empire of Magnetism* (Oxford UP, 2024).
- Naylor, *The Observatory Experiment: Meteorology in Britain and Its Empire* (Cambridge UP, 2024).

### D20 Computer Science — D3 Population/people/language classification → **1**

Censuses and classification systems clearly helped drive punched-card and data-processing infrastructures. Yet the canonical mechanization path is dominated by metropolitan U.S./British census administration, followed by postwar military/state/commercial computing; colonial census systems are a secondary diffusion/application channel. Computer science itself institutionalized mainly after World War II. Therefore the colonial-classification connection is documented but peripheral.

Key evidence:
- Campbell-Kelly, “Information Technology and Organizational Change in the British Census, 1801–1911,” *Information Systems Research* 7(1), 1996, DOI 10.1287/isre.7.1.22.
- U.S. Census Bureau histories of Hollerith tabulation and UNIVAC.
- Christopher (2008) and related histories documenting colonial census/classification systems.
