# ARIS4C003 — Independent Coder B IKES Raw Response

Independent second historical coder (Coder B). Coding of the Imperial/Colonial
Knowledge Entanglement Score (IKES) for the 21 frozen conceptual disciplines,
per `WORKBUDDY_HANDOFF.md`. Scores reflect only historical scholarly evidence of
imperial/colonial systems materially shaping each discipline's institutional
formation, methods, objects/data, training, infrastructure, or postcolonial
continuation. No Coder A scores and no contemporary outcomes/rankings were
consulted.

## Part 2 — Evidence sections

### D01 — Anthropology
Anthropology's institutional formation is deeply entangled with colonial
administration: British social anthropology in particular grew through the
colonial service and bodies such as the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute and the
African Survey. Ethnographic fieldwork was overwhelmingly conducted in colonies
(D4=3), and racial/ethnic and linguistic classification (D3=3) was central to
the discipline's data infrastructure. Ethnographic museums (D9=3) and missionary
linguistic documentation (D10=3) supplied its core collections and texts, while
postwar area studies and development anthropology (D11=3) continued the
relationship. The administrative-demand role (D1=3) is foundational; territorial
survey (D2) and resource extraction (D5) were peripheral.
Sources: Stocking, *Victorian Anthropology* (1987) and *Race, Culture, and
Evolution* (1968); Kuklick, *The Savage Within* (1991); Asad (ed.), *Anthropology
& the Colonial Encounter* (1973); Tilley, *Africa as a Living Laboratory*
(2011).
Uncertainty: strongest in British/French empires; varies by national tradition
(US four-field anthropology less directly administrative). No dimension scored NA.
Confidence: high

### D02 — Archaeology
Colonial archaeology supplied the collections and legitimizing narratives for
imperial museums (D9=3) and relied on expeditions across colonized territories
(D4=3). It served colonial antiquities administration (D1=2) and territorial
mapping of sites (D2=2), with ethnographic/racial framing of antiquities (D3=2).
Missionary biblical archaeology contributed marginally (D10=1). Postcolonial
nation-building repurposed archaeology (D11=2). Direct links to tropical
medicine (D6) or plantations (D7) are absent.
Sources: Trigger, *A History of Archaeological Thought* (2006); Shepherd,
"Archaeology and Apartheid" (1993) and Mudimbe, *The Invention of Africa*
(1988) on colonial knowledge.
Uncertainty: strong in Middle East/North Africa/South Asia; national variation.
D6 and D7 scored 0 (no credible imperial-entanglement role).
Confidence: medium

### D03 — Geography
Geography was a quintessential imperial science: mapping, boundary-making, and
environmental description (D2=3) were tasks for empire, and geographical
societies were patronage hubs (D1=3). Exploration and expeditions (D4=2),
resource/economic mapping (D5=2), census/demographic mapping (D3=2), and
environmental/landscape description (D7=2) all drew on colonial contexts.
Postwar development/regional geography (D11=3) continued the tradition.
Sources: Livingstone, *The Geographical Tradition* (1992); Driver, *Geography
Militant* (2001); Godlewska & Smith (eds.), *Geography and Empire* (1994).
Uncertainy: the "imperial science" thesis is well established; variation by
subfield. No NA dimensions.
Confidence: high

### D04 — Development Studies
Development Studies is best read as the direct postwar institutional
continuation of colonial development administration (D11=3; D1=3): colonial
development departments, the 1929 Colonial Development Act, and late-colonial
planning mutated into modernization theory and aid machinery. Demographic and
fieldwork components (D3=2, D4=2) and agricultural/land reform work (D7=2)
persist, as does institution-building (D8=2). Museums/collections play no role
(D9=0).
Sources: Cowen & Shenton, *Doctrines of Development* (1996); Escobar,
*Encountering Development* (1995); Hodge, *Triumph of the Expert* (2007) on late
colonial development.
Uncertainty: the discipline is largely a postwar creation, so "formation" is
continuous with colonial governance rather than separate. No NA.
Confidence: high

### D05 — Linguistics
Linguistics is entangled primarily through classification of colonized peoples'
languages (D3=3) and missionary documentation (D10=3): missionaries and colonial
officials produced the first descriptive grammars and language surveys.
Administrative language policy (D1=2), fieldwork (D4=2), education/translation
policy (D8=2), and postwar literacy/language planning (D11=2) extend the link.
Resource extraction (D5) and medicine (D6/D7) are absent.
Sources: Errington, *Linguistics in a Colonial World* (2008); Fabian, *Language
and Colonial Power* (1986); Raj, *Beyond Postcolonialism* (2003).
Uncertainty: strongest in Africa/Asia/Pacific; weaker for philologically oriented
European linguistics. D5, D6, D7 scored 0.
Confidence: high

### D06 — Tropical Medicine / colonial-health Public Health
This field is among the most directly imperial: it was created to govern
colonial populations and mobile European bodies (D1=3, D6=3), with field
research stations across the tropics (D4=3) and racial/epidemiological
framing (D3=2). Plantation/military/mission health extended the network
(D5=1, D10=2), and postwar WHO/global-health aid (D11=3) continues it. Disease
mapping (D2=1) and public-health administration (D8=2) are supporting roles.
Sources: Vaughan, *Curing their Ills* (1991); Arnold (ed.), *Imperial Medicine
and Indigenous Societies* (1988); Anderson, *Colonial Pathologies* (2006);
Worboys, "Imperial Tropical Medicine" (1996); Curtin, *The Image of Africa*
(1964).
Uncertainty: vigorous debate on whether tropical medicine served extraction or
care, but entanglement itself is unambiguous. No NA.
Confidence: high

### D07 — Agriculture & Forestry
Agriculture and forestry were imperial sciences of rule: colonial agricultural
departments and experimental stations (D1=3, D4=3) drove crop transfer,
botanic gardens, plantation ecology, and forestry management (D7=3). Land/soil
survey (D2=2), economic botany/resource inventories (D5=2), botanical
collections (D9=2), and agricultural colleges/extension (D8=2) supported it;
postwar Green Revolution/FAO work (D11=3) is the continuity.
Sources: Grove, *Green Imperialism* (1995); Rajan, *Modernizing Nature* (2006);
Sivaramakrishnan, *Modern Forests* (1999); Richards, *The Unending Frontier*
(2003).
Uncertainty: variation between settler and extractive colonies; strong
documentation. No NA.
Confidence: high

### D08 — Geology / Earth-resource sciences
Geology's imperial role centers on territorial and resource mapping: geological
surveys and the Great Trigonometrical Survey (D2=3), mineral/resource
inventories for extraction (D5=3), and expeditionary fieldwork (D4=2).
Administrative demand (D1=2), geological museum collections (D9=2), and postwar
resource development (D11=2) extend it. Population classification (D3) and
missionary networks (D10) are absent.
Sources: Rudwick, *Geologies* (2005) and *Bursting the Limits of Time* (2005);
Keay, *The Great Arc* (2000); pyenson & sheetz-nishimura on colonial science
networks.
Uncertainty: well documented for British/Indian surveys; less for some empires.
D3 and D10 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D09 — Sociology
Sociology's colonial entanglement is real but weaker than anthropology's: urban
and village community studies often used colonial settings (D4=2), social surveys
served administration (D1=2), and census/social statistics (D3=2) and rural
sociology (D7=1) drew on imperial contexts. The discipline's core formation was
more metropolitan (industrial society) than colonial, so museums (D9) and
medicine (D6) links are negligible.
Sources: K. Kumar, *The Making of English National Identity*; for colonial
social surveys see colonial census/commission literature; contrast with
anthropology's tighter bond (Asad 1973).
Uncertainty: the link is indirect and nationally variable; some traditions
(European) barely colonial. D5, D6, D9 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D10 — Political Science / International Relations
Direct imperial entanglement is modest. Comparative/constitutional study drew on
colonial administration (D1=2) and law/Institutions were exported (D8=2);
postwar area studies, modernization theory, and IR (D11=3) inherited colonial
knowledge networks, and some fieldwork occurred in the Global South (D4=1).
Population classification, resource, medicine, museums, and missionary links are
absent.
Sources: for area studies' roots see (e.g.) critiques in development/area
literature; for colonial administration's influence on comparative politics,
colonial-service training records. The discipline's formative core is
post-1945 and metropolitan.
Uncertainty: strong debate; the construct here scores the discipline's formation
low on direct imperial mechanisms. D2, D3, D5, D6, D7, D9, D10 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D11 — Law
Law is strongly entangled through colonial legal transplantation: received laws,
codification, and legal pluralism (D8=3) were instruments of rule, and colonial
law/administration shaped the discipline's formation (D1=3). Postwar "law and
development" (D11=3) and land/property law (D7=1) continue it; status-of-persons
law touches classification (D3=1), missionary/mission-law schools (D10=1).
Territorial survey, field sites, resource, medicine, and museums are not
mechanisms here.
Sources: Benton, *Law and Colonial Cultures* (2002); Mamdani, *Citizen and
Subject* (1996); Merry, "Colonialism, law, and the social sciences" (1992).
Uncertainty: civil- vs common-law empires differ, but transplantation is
universal. D2, D4, D5, D6, D9 scored 0.
Confidence: high

### D12 — Economics
Economics' colonial link is moderate: colonial economic administration and
resource/plantation economics (D1=2, D5=2), agricultural economics (D7=2), and
postwar development economics (D11=3) are the main channels. Demographic and
fieldwork components (D3=1, D4=1) and department-building (D8=1) are minor. The
discipline's neoclassical core is metropolitan and abstract, so museums,
medicine, and missionary links are absent.
Sources: for colonial economic administration see Cain & Hopkins, *British
Imperialism* (1993); for development economics' colonial lineage, Toye,
*Dilemmas of Development* (1987).
Uncertainty: strong for development/agricultural economics, weak for theory.
D6, D9, D10 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D13 — Public Administration / Social Policy
This field is essentially the administrative apparatus of empire continued:
colonial civil services, district administration, and the "science of
administration" (D1=3, D8=3) shaped it, and postwar public administration in
development (D11=3) is the direct continuation. Census/social-policy data (D3=1),
health administration (D6=1), and mission administration (D10=1) are supporting.
Territorial survey, field sites, resource, agriculture, and museums are not
central mechanisms.
Sources: for colonial administration as discipline-formation see classical
administrative histories (e.g., colonial civil-service literature) and
Hutchins, *The Illusion of Permanence* (1967) on educational/admin legacies.
Uncertainty: the discipline is often housed in policy schools with strong
postcolonial continuities. D2, D4, D5, D7, D9 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D14 — Education
Education was a primary colonial instrument: colonial education departments
(D1=3), exported school systems (D8=3), and mission schools (D10=3) built the
field; postwar education-development aid (D11=3) continues it. Curriculum,
literacy, and education-census work (D3=1, D4=1, D6=1, D7=1) are supporting.
Territorial survey, field sites, and museums are not mechanisms.
Sources: for mission education see (e.g.) King, *Pan-Africanism and Education*
(1971) and mission-schooling histories; for colonial education departments,
Whitehead and colonial-policy literature.
Uncertainty: strongest where mission/state schooling was extensive (Africa,
India); variation by colony. D2, D4, D9 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D15 — History
History's imperial entanglement is moderate and mostly indirect: imperial/colonial
history is a major subfield, archives and colonial records are central to the
discipline's practice (D9=3), and colonial settings supplied field sites for
archive-based research (D4=2) and postwar area/Postcolonial history (D11=2).
Administrative historiography (D1=1) and historical geography (D2=1) are minor;
missionary history (D10=1) marginal. Population classification, resource,
medicine, agriculture, and law channels are not central.
Sources: Dirks (ed.), *Colonialism and Culture* (1992); Stoler, *Along the
Archival Grain* (2009) on colonial archives; the colonial archive is itself a
disciplinary infrastructure.
Uncertainty: the discipline studies empire but was not "formed" by it as
strongly as the imperial sciences. No NA.
Confidence: medium

### D16 — Demography / Population Studies
Demography is entangled through the census and vital statistics: colonial
censuses and population classification (D3=3) were instruments of rule, and
population administration (D1=2) and postwar population-and-development (D11=3)
continue it. Survey work (D4=1), health/demographic surveillance (D6=1), and
administrative demography (D8=1, D10=1) are supporting. Territorial survey,
resource, agriculture, and museums are not mechanisms.
Sources: Cohn, "The census, social structure and objectification in India"
(1990); Kertzer & Arel (eds.), *Census and Identity* (2002) on colonial census
classification.
Uncertainty: the modern discipline is also metropolitan; the colonial census is
the clearest link. D2, D5, D7, D9 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D17 — Mathematics
Mathematics shows weak direct imperial entanglement: it was taught in colonial
universities and used in surveying/navigation/standards (D1=1, D2=1, D8=1,
D10=1, D11=1), but its formation and objects are not colonially constituted. No
field-site, resource, medicine, agriculture, or museum mechanisms apply.
Sources: general histories of mathematics note imperial diffusion of training
(Katz, *Mathematics in India*; colonial college histories) but no imperial
shaping of the discipline's content.
Uncertainty: some imperial diffusion of mathematical training exists, but not
formation-level entanglement. D4, D5, D6, D7, D9 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D18 — Physics
Physics has minimal direct imperial entanglement: imperial observatories,
geodetic/standards work (D2=1, D4=1, D8=1, D11=1, D1=1) supplied some
infrastructure, but the discipline's formation is metropolitan and
universalistic. No population, resource, medicine, agriculture, museum, or
missionary mechanisms apply.
Sources: histories of imperial observatories (e.g., surveys of colonial
astronomy/geodesy) note peripheral roles; no formation-level dependence.
Uncertainty: some colonial observatories existed, but entanglement is weak. D3,
D5, D6, D7, D9, D10 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D19 — Chemistry
Chemistry's modest link is through extraction-related work: mining/metallurgy and
economic-botany chemistry (D5=2), plus agricultural/soil chemistry (D7=1),
pharmaceutical-adjacent work (D6=1), and colonial college training (D1=1, D8=1,
D11=1). Population classification, field sites, museums, and missionary links are
absent.
Sources: histories of colonial mining/assay and agricultural chemistry note
resource-driven demand; no formation-level dependence on empire.
Uncertainty: applied colonial chemistry existed but did not shape the discipline.
D3, D4, D9, D10 scored 0.
Confidence: medium

### D20 — Computer Science
Computer Science is essentially post-colonial in formation: it emerged mid-20th
century after formal empire ended, so direct imperial entanglement is negligible.
Only university training diffusion (D8=1) and postwar technical-assistance
continuity (D11=1) are plausibly traceable; all administrative, survey,
population, field-site, resource, medicine, agriculture, museum, and missionary
mechanisms score 0.
Sources: the discipline's historiography (e.g., histories of computing) places
its institutional birth in post-1945 industrialized states; no colonial
formation mechanism is documented.
Uncertainty: any link is indirect and late; scoring is deliberately minimal. D1,
D2, D3, D4, D5, D6, D7, D9, D10 scored 0.
Confidence: high

### D21 — Materials Science
Materials Science, like chemistry/physics, shows limited direct entanglement:
extraction/metallurgy relevance (D5=2), standards/training diffusion (D1=1,
D8=1, D11=1), and material-transfer/ecological management (D7=1) are the only
channels. Population, survey, field-site, medicine, museum, and missionary
mechanisms do not apply.
Sources: colonial mining/metallurgical demand is documented in economic-history
literature, but the discipline's formation is post-war and metropolitan.
Uncertainty: applied colonial materials work existed without shaping the field.
D2, D3, D4, D6, D9, D10 scored 0.
Confidence: medium
