## Part 2

### D01 — Anthropology

The mechanisms here are institutional rather than attitudinal: colonial administrative demand produced funded fieldwork pipelines and a canon of objects, and the discipline's central taxonomic problem ("tribe", "caste", "primitive society") was constituted out of census, gazetteer and museum material rather than imported into it. British social anthropology is the strongest national case, with the International African Institute and its journal *Africa* (1928), *African Political Systems* (Fortes & Evans-Pritchard eds., Oxford UP for the IAI, 1940), the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute (1937–1964) and the Sudan Political Service forming a continuous state–institute–chair circuit; the French Dakar–Djibouti mission (1931–33), Leiden's KITLV (1851) and Tervuren (1897) show the same pattern outside Britain. D9 is scored 3 because the discipline's museum base — Pitt Rivers, Trocadéro/Musée de l'Homme, Berlin, Leiden — did not merely store illustrations of theory but supplied the comparison itself. D2, D5 and D7 are low deliberately: ethnographic mapping and "primitive economy" studies are documented but peripheral, and I did not score the well-attested colonial *use* of ethnographers as disciplinary formation.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): George W. Stocking Jr. ed., *Colonial Situations: Essays on the Contextualization of Ethnographic Knowledge*, History of Anthropology vol. 7 (U. of Wisconsin Press, 1991); Bernard S. Cohn, *Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India* (Princeton UP, 1996); Nicholas B. Dirks, *Castes of Mind: Colonialism and the Making of Modern India* (Princeton UP, 2001); Henrika Kuklick, *The Savage Within: The Social History of British Anthropology, 1785–1945* (Cambridge UP, 1991); Talal Asad ed., *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (I.B. Tauris/Dept. of Anthropology, U. of London, 1973); D. H. Johnson, "Evans-Pritchard, the Nuer, and the Sudan Political Service," *African Affairs* 81(323) (1982), 231–246; Leroy Vail ed., *The Creation of Tribalism in Southern Africa* (James Currey/CODESRIA, 1989); Andrew Zimmerman, *Anthropology and Antihumanism in Imperial Germany* (U. of Chicago Press, 2008).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: metropolitan Victorian evolutionism (Tylor, Morgan, Frazer) predates the 1880s "new imperialism" and worked from compilations, not fieldwork, so the D3/D9 findings should not be read backwards into the whole nineteenth century. US anthropology is a genuine counter-case — Boasian four-field formation through Columbia, the AMNH and salvage ethnography on Indigenous land, then Cold-War national-security funding — which is settler-colonial rather than overseas-colonial and is a different mechanism. American and Scandinavian material is thinner than the British, French and Dutch; Portuguese colonial ethnography rests on institutional knowledge (Junta de Investigações do Ultramar, *Missões Culturais*) for which I could not verify a monograph, and Japanese imperial ethnology (Torii Ryūzō, Yanagita, Korea/Okinawa/Manchuria) is documented only at article level in my searches. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: high

### D02 — Archaeology

Colonial state archaeological services *were* the discipline's institutional form across much of the world: the Archaeological Survey of India (Cunningham 1861; Marshall 1902–28), the Egyptian Service des Antiquités (Mariette, 1858), the Department of Antiquities of Mandatory Palestine (1920), the Oudheidkundige Dienst in Nederlandsch-Indië (Batavia, 1913) and the Service des Antiquités de la Syrie et du Liban set excavation permits, defined which sites counted, trained local staff, and controlled the division of finds. D4 and D9 are scored 3 together because expedition societies (PEF 1865, EEF 1882, IFAO 1880, École française d'Extrême-Orient 1900, Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft 1898, ASOR 1900, Peabody/Carnegie Mesoamerican programmes) and imperial museums jointly manufactured both the field method and the material canon. The divide-of-finds system is scored under D5 as extraction with disciplinary consequences, not merely as looting rhetoric. D11 is 2 rather than 3 because post-independence successor departments (ASI, Iraq's State Board of Antiquities and Heritage, Egypt's Antiquities Organization, Syria's DG-AM) retained colonial infrastructure and canons, which is continuity of administration more than of research programme.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Bruce G. Trigger, *A History of Archaeological Thought*, 2nd ed. (Cambridge UP, 2006); Donald Malcolm Reid, *Whose Pharaohs? Archaeology, Museums, and Egyptian National Identity from Napoleon to World War I* (U. of California Press, 2002); Margarita Díaz-Andreu, *A World History of Nineteenth-Century Archaeology: Nationalism, Colonialism, and the Past* (Oxford UP, 2007); Chris Gosden, *Archaeology and Colonialism: Cultural Contact from 5000 BC to the Present* (Cambridge UP, 2004); Lynn Meskell ed., *Archaeology Under Fire* (Routledge, 1998) and *A Future in Ruins: UNESCO, World Heritage, and the Dream of Peace* (Oxford UP, 2018); Ashish Avikunthak, *Bureaucratic Archaeology: State, Science, and Past in Postcolonial India* (Cambridge UP, 2021); Stephen L. Dyson, *In Pursuit of Ancient Pasts* (Yale UP, 2006); Peter R. Schmidt ed., *Postcolonial Archaeologies in Africa* (SAR Press, 2009).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: European prehistory and classical archaeology — Copenhagen's three-age system, the German research-institute tradition, philhellenism, stratigraphic training in Wales and Scotland — are metropolitan formations, so a high general score would overstate the case for the whole discipline; the strongest claim is region-specific (Egypt, Mesopotamia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Mandate Palestine). US hemispheric archaeology is better described as frontier/settler-colonial than overseas-colonial, and post-1945 expansion (radiocarbon, NSF, cultural-resource management) is a rival driver. Italian Libya and Japanese Korea are attested institutionally but I found no comparable scholarly synthesis for Portuguese or Belgian colonial archaeology. NA dimensions: D7 (agriculture/forestry/ecological transfer) — the connection between colonial botanical infrastructure and site conservation is plausible but I could not verify a source that treats it as disciplinary formation, and per the handoff I have not converted that gap into a zero.

Confidence: high

### D03 — Geography

This is the clearest discipline-wide case in the set, because the objects, methods and employment structure all sit inside the colonial state at once: the Great Trigonometrical Survey and the Survey of India, the Royal Geographical Society's Diploma (from 1881) training colonial schoolteachers, the Colonial Research Committee (1940) and Colonial Social Science Research Council (1944) funding regional survey, and Isaiah Bowman mapping mandates at Versailles. D2 and D4 are 3 on Edney's monographic account of the GTS as an imperial epistemic apparatus and Driver's account of expeditionary culture as the discipline's public method; D1 is 3 because chairs and readerships (Mackinder at Oxford and LSE, Vidal de la Blache at Lyon from 1898, the Institut de géographie coloniale at the Sorbonne 1921, the Institut colonial de Bordeaux) were created to serve colonial demand. Unusually among the 21, six dimensions reach 2 or above with independent monographic support.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Matthew H. Edney, *Mapping an Empire: The Geographical Construction of British India, 1765–1843* (U. of Chicago Press, 1997); Felix Driver, *Geography Militant: Cultures of Exploration and Empire* (Blackwell, 2001); David N. Livingstone, *The Geographical Tradition: Episodes in the History of a Contested Enterprise* (Blackwell, 1992); Thomas J. Bassett, "Cartography and Empire Building in Nineteenth-Century West Africa," *Geographical Review* 84(3) (1994), 316–335, and *Writing the Land: French Imperial and Colonial Mapping of West Africa, 1854–1892* (U. of Chicago Press, 2024); Pierre Singaravélou, *L'Empire des géographes: géographie, exploration et colonisation (XIXe–XXe siècle)* (Belin, 2007); Richard H. Grove, *Green Imperialism* (Cambridge UP, 1995); Mark Harrison, *Climates and Constitutions* (Oxford UP, 1999); James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (Yale UP, 1998), on scientific forestry as a state-legibility technique.

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: national heterogeneity is severe and I have deliberately not flattened it. The German Humboldt–Ritter–Ratzel core was formed in the continental *Universität* and *Länderkunde* tradition, and Germany's overseas empire (1884–1918) was too short to have constituted it; France's *pays*/genre de vie tradition was shaped by metropolitan regional monographs and by the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, with colonial geography running as a parallel subfield; American geography's twentieth-century formation (Hartshorne 1939, the Quantitative Revolution, NSF/ONR/RAND money) is a strong discontinuity case. Dutch, Belgian and Portuguese colonial geography are institutionally established (Royal Dutch Geographical Society 1873, Société Royale Belge de Géographie 1876 and Leopold's Congo, Sociedade de Geografia de Lisboa 1875 and the Pink Map) but rest partly on institutional histories rather than peer-reviewed syntheses; Japanese colonial geography in Korea and Manchuria (Mantetsu land-classification surveys) I could verify only weakly. D6 (medical geography) carries a 2 partly on adjacent evidence; Livingstone's article on medicine and the "Geographical Tradition" could not be confirmed at journal level. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: high

### D04 — Development Studies

The verified evidence is dense on colonial development *practice and doctrine* and much thinner on colonial development *discipline*: the objects, categories and personnel pipelines of the field demonstrably come from late-colonial development offices, but the university field itself is a 1960s invention built under US, philanthropic and multilateral auspices. Colonial administrative demand, the extraction/commodity-export core, agrarian doctrine and postwar continuity are all documented: Cooper on the late-colonial development offensive in both British and French Africa, Hodge on agrarian doctrines as the direct ancestry of development practice, Tignor on W. Arthur Lewis's canonical dual-economy model being generated from a Caribbean plantation economy, and Wagner on imperial-research governance running explicitly to 1982. I have capped this at 1.45 rather than scoring the field at the top: D2 and D10 have no credible evidence at discipline level, and the strongest institutional link — the Imperial College of Tropical Agriculture, Trinidad (1922), the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute, Makerere, and the International Institute of Social Studies, The Hague (1952, founded to train personnel for the Indies) — is better documented by institutional histories than by peer-reviewed disciplinary history.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Frederick Cooper, *Decolonization and African Society: The Labor Question in French and British Africa* (Cambridge UP, 1996); Joseph M. Hodge, *Triumph of the Expert: Agrarian Doctrines of Development and the Legacies of British Colonialism* (Ohio U. Press, 2007); Florian Wagner, *Colonial Internationalism and the Governmentality of Empire, 1893–1982* (Cambridge UP, 2022); Robert L. Tignor, *W. Arthur Lewis and the Birth of Development Economics* (Princeton UP, 2020); Allen F. Isaacman & Barbara S. Isaacman, *Dams, Displacement, and the Delusion of Development: Cahora Bassa and its Legacies in Mozambique, 1965–2007* (Ohio U. Press, 2013); Stephen G. Macekura & Erez Manela eds., *The Development Century: A Global History* (Cambridge UP, 2018); Aram Ziai, *Development Discourse and Global History* (Routledge, 2015); Sabine Clarke, "The Research Council System and the Politics of Medical and Health Research in Late Colonial Kenya," *Medical History* 57(3) (2013), 338–358.

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: this is the most genuinely contested cell-level reading in the matrix. The counter-thesis is strong and specifically documented — Nick Cullather, "Development? It's History," *Diplomatic History* 24(4) (2000), 641–653, and David L. Szanton ed., *The Politics of Knowledge: Area Studies and the Disciplines* (U. of California Press, 2002) — and the Latin American structuralist lineage (Prebisch/ECLAC) is semi-peripheral and anti-colonial-office, complicating any single origin story. The British tradition supports the colonial-continuity reading best; the US tradition supports discontinuity; the Dutch rests on ISS/ASC-Leiden; French *mise en valeur* and FIDES remain a gap in my evidence base for lack of a verified Anglophone or Francophone monograph. The discursive classics (Escobar, *Encountering Development*, 1995; Sachs ed., *The Development Dictionary*, 1992; Rist, *The History of Development*, 1997) establish knowledge-power genealogy, which I have treated as weaker than institutional evidence and therefore as supporting 2 rather than 3. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: medium

### D05 — Linguistics

Two dimensions are foundational here while the theoretical core is not, and the split is the finding rather than an inconvenience. Colonial classification is the discipline's signature entanglement (D3 = 3): the Linguistic Survey of India (1898–1928) was a Government of India undertaking with paid provincial correspondents and official publication, and Meinhof's "ethnoclassification" fused racial and linguistic taxonomy at Hamburg, so the census-language column and the survey return became the discipline's data infrastructure; missionary and religious networks (D10 = 3) were the single largest historical supplier of grammars, dictionaries, orthographies and literacy practice, and SIL/*Ethnologue* continue that line. At the same time the discipline's own narrated lineage is metropolitan and self-sufficient, and I have not scored colonial grammars as disciplinary formation where they were produced by missionaries for evangelisation.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): James Errington, *Linguistics in a Colonial World: A Story of Language, Meaning, and Power* (Wiley-Blackwell, 2008); Javed Majeed, *Colonialism and Knowledge in Grierson's Linguistic Survey of India* (Routledge, 2019); Philip Jagessar, "Correspondence, scale and the Linguistic Survey of India's colonial geographies of language, 1896–1928," *Journal of Historical Geography* 84 (2024), 1–13; H. Ekkehard Wolff ed., *A History of African Linguistics* (Cambridge UP, 2019), ch. 2 "Western Europe: African Linguistics and the Colonial Project"; Sara Pugach, *Africa in Translation: A History of Colonial Linguistics in Germany and Beyond, 1814–1945* (U. of Michigan Press, 2015); Ana Deumert, Anne Storch & Nick Shepherd eds., *Colonial and Decolonial Linguistics: Histories, Materials, Methods* (Oxford UP, 2020); Klaus Zimmermann & Johanna Kellermeier-Rehbein eds., *Colonialism and Missionary Linguistics* (De Gruyter Mouton, 2015); William F. Hanks, *Converting Words: Maya in the Age of the Cross* (U. of California Press, 2010); Ashraf Abdelhay, Busi Makoni & Sinfree Makoni, "The colonial linguistics of governance in Sudan: the Rejaf Language Conference, 1928," *Journal of African Cultural Studies* 28(3) (2016), 343–358; Bernard S. Cohn, *Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge* (Princeton UP, 1996).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: the strongest disconfirming evidence is that Saussurean structure, Prague phonology, Copenhagen glossematics, Firthian London and Chomskyan syntax were built from Greek–Latin–Sanskrit philology and intra-European debate — R. H. Robins, *A Short History of Linguistics*, remains the standard statement of that lineage, and its persistence in the syllabus is itself evidence of how the discipline narrates itself. American structuralism's colonial object was domestic (Bureau of American Ethnology, Boasian salvage under allotment and boarding-school policy), which is a settler-colonial mechanism I have deliberately not merged with overseas colonialism, and Boas was politically anti-colonial. The colonial/decolonial-linguistics literature is internally contested — several contributors in Deumert–Storch–Shepherd treat coloniality as a framing rather than as constitutive. National coverage is uneven: British (India, IAC/IAI, SOAS) and German (Hamburg) are best documented; Dutch Indies language policy, Spanish-American colonial grammar traditions and Japanese Korea/Okinawa/Hokkaido classification I could only partially verify, and INALCO's colonial role is unconfirmed. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: medium

### D06 — Tropical Medicine / colonial-health-related Public Health

This is the set's most directly evidenced institutional case. The specialty was created out of imperial demand and imperial money — the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine (1898), the first in the world, and the London School of Tropical Medicine (1899), with Liverpool running field expeditions to Gambia in 1902 under Dutton and Ross — and its defining methods (clinical and parasitological survey, expedition medicine, entomological mapping, mass chemoprophylaxis) were developed in colonial settings, so D1, D4 and D11 take 3. D6 is also 3, which requires an explicit caveat: for this discipline D6 is close to definitional, so it should not be treated as independent corroboration of the pattern; a robustness check that drops D6 moves this row's mean from 2.27 to 2.20, and Coder A's treatment of self-referential dimensions is worth comparing on that basis.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Philip D. Curtin, *Imperial Medicine: Patrick Manson and the Conquest of Tropical Disease* (U. of Pennsylvania Press, 1985) and "Medical Knowledge and Urban Planning in Tropical Africa," *American Historical Review* 90(3) (1985); Philip D. Curtin, *Death by Migration: Europe's Encounter with the Tropical World in the Nineteenth Century* (Cambridge UP, 1989); David Arnold, *Colonising the Body: State Medicine and Epidemic Disease in Nineteenth-Century India* (Oxford UP / U. of California Press, 1993); Randall M. Packard, *The Making of a Tropical Disease: A Short History of Malaria* (Duke UP, 2007); B. G. Maegraith, "History of the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine," *Medical History* 16 (1972), 354–368; G. C. Cook, "'Malaria Liverpool': an illustrated history of the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine," *Transactions of the Royal Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene* 94(3) (2000); Megan Vaughan, "A Research Enclave in 1940s Nigeria: The Rockefeller Foundation Yellow Fever Research Institute at Yaba, Lagos, 1943–49," *Bulletin of the History of Medicine* 92(1) (2018), 172–205; H. Cho & M. Robert, "Medical schools in empires: connecting the dots," *Medical History* 68(2) (2024), 110–127; Myriam Mertens & Guillaume Lachenal, "The History of 'Belgian' Tropical Medicine from a Cross-Border Perspective," *Revue belge de Philologie et d'Histoire* 90(4) (2012), 1249–1271.

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: the discipline name bundles a specialty (tropical medicine) with a field (public health), and the two score differently. Mark Harrison, *Public Health in British India: Anglo-Indian Preventive Medicine, 1859–1914* (Cambridge UP, 1994) is the essential limiting monograph — colonial preventive medicine was thin, contested and fiscally starved — and Simon Szreter, "The Importance of Social Intervention in Britain's Mortality Decline c.1850–1914," *Social History of Medicine* 1(1) (1988), 1–37, shows Britain's mortality decline driven by domestic sanitary and social intervention, with its public-health institutions growing from Chadwick, the Poor Law and municipal reform. Tropical specialists were themselves marginal to British academic medicine, so the specialty was simultaneously empire-made and empire-neglected. The *Networks in Tropical Medicine* monograph (Stanford UP, 2012) I could verify as title/publisher/year but not confidently as to authorship, so I have not leaned on it. National variation is broad and well evidenced — British, French (Au on Cambodia; Cho & Robert on Pondicherry/Hanoi), Portuguese (Cagle; also "The emergence of tropical medicine in Portugal," *Dynamis* 28, 2008), Belgian, Dutch, German, US (Rockefeller IHD, Philippines) and Japanese (Liu on Taiwan/Korea; Akami on the LNHO). D10 (missionary networks) is 1 because mission hospitals as a data source and recruitment pool are plausible but thinly verified for medicine specifically. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: high

### D07 — Agriculture & Forestry

D7 takes 3 because crop transfer, botanic-garden networks, agricultural stations and state forestry are the discipline's substance as much as its history: Grove on tropical island "edens" and the origins of systematic nature protection, Brockway on Kew as an administrative-economic instrument with seed and bulletin circulation, and Schiebinger & Swan on European knowledge-forms being constituted by colonial commerce rather than merely applied to it. D11 takes 3 because the colonial-to-development bridge is documented institutionally rather than rhetorically — the Imperial Institute (1893) to Tropical Products Institute (1958) to Natural Resources Institute chain, CD&W-funded commodity research, the ICAR (1929) taken over intact by the Indian state, and Byerlee & Lynam's pre-history of the CGIAR built on interwar tropical stations. D8 takes 2 on the strongest single feedback loop I found anywhere in this exercise: Brandis taught Pinchot at the Bilt Forest School in India, and Pinchot then founded professional US forestry.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Richard H. Grove, *Green Imperialism* (Cambridge UP, 1995); H. Allan Brockway Jr., *Science and Colonial Expansion: The Role of the British Royal Botanic Gardens* (Academic Press, 1979); Londa Schiebinger & Claudia Swan eds., *Colonial Botany: Science, Commerce, and Politics in the Early Modern World* (U. of Pennsylvania Press, 2005); Richard Drayton, *Nature's Government: Science, Imperial Britain, and the "Improvement" of the World* (Yale UP, 2000); Gregory Barton, *Empire Forestry and the Origins of Environmentalism* (Cambridge UP, 2001) and *Modernizing Nature: Forestry and Imperial Eco-Development 1800–1950* (Oxford UP, 2008); Gregory Barton, "Empire Forestry and American Environmentalism," *Environment and History* 6(1) (2000); K. Sivaramakrishnan, *Modern Forests: Statemaking and Environmental Change in Colonial Eastern India* (Stanford UP, 1999); Harro Maat, *Science Cultivating Practice: A History of Agricultural Science in the Netherlands and its Colonies, 1863–1986* (Kluwer, 2001); Sabine Clarke, *Science at the End of Empire: Experts and the Development of the British Caribbean, 1940–62* (Manchester UP, 2018); Derek Byerlee & John K. Lynam, "The development of the international center model for agricultural research: A prehistory of the CGIAR," *World Development* 135 (2020), 105080; Helen Anne Curry & Timothy W. Lorek eds., *Agricultural Science as International Development* (Cambridge UP, 2024).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: two strong non-colonial cradles limit the row. Scientific forestry's method is central-European — Carlowitz's *Sylvicultura Oeconomica* (1713), sustained yield and working plans, the Saxon/Freiberg schools, per Joachim Radkau, *The Invention of Sustainability* (Cambridge UP, 2017), ch. 5 — making colonial tropical forestry partly derivative application with a documented feedback loop. Agricultural science's other cradle is metropolitan and domestic-political: the German station movement (Liebig/Thaer, on which John L. Finlay's work is standard) and the US Morrill Act (1862) and Hatch Act (1887) state-station system. The US is therefore qualified rather than exempt, since the USDA Office of Experiment Stations ran tropical stations in Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines and Guam after 1898. Dutch integration of metropole and colony is the strongest single-nation case on Maat; French *Eaux et Forêts* in Indochine and AOF, and postcolonial continuity in Malaysia (RRIM/PORIM), Sri Lanka, Indonesia (Bogor→IPB) and Zimbabwe are attested by articles whose authors I could not always retrieve. D3, D6 and D10 are low because the relevant mechanisms (forest-classification, plantation-labour health, missionary schools of agriculture) belong analytically to other disciplines or to history of medicine. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: high

### D08 — Geology / Earth-resource sciences

D5 is the row's core channel and takes 3: the current field-level statement of the historiography treats empire, extraction, expeditions and collections as constitutive of earth science — Rieppel's chapter "Earth Science and Extractive Capitalism in the Age of Empire" and Felten's "Mining and the Formation of Modern Geology" in the Aronova/Sepkoski/Tamborini *Handbook* (Springer, 2024) — and Bhalai's monograph on Jamaica gives the cleanest institutional biography of a colonial survey becoming a postcolonial one. D11 takes 3 because the colonial→postcolonial handover in geology was near-total in institutional form: the Geological Survey of India, the *Geologische Dienst* becoming Indonesia's *Djawatan Geologi*, the Nigerian and Malayan surveys, and France's *Corps des Mines*→BRGM *Bureau minier de la France d'outre-mer* brigades in Africa, with Hecht's uranium analysis showing the chain persisting through decolonisation.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Elena Aronova, David Sepkoski & Marco Tamborini eds., *Handbook of the Historiography of the Earth and Environmental Sciences* (Springer, 2024), incl. Lukas Rieppel, "Earth Science and Extractive Capitalism in the Age of Empire," and chapters by Sebastian Felten and Marianne Klemun; Suresh Bhalai, *The History of Mining and Geological Surveys in Jamaica* (Springer, 2023); Aja B. Tolman, "Geologists and the British Raj, 1870–1910" (PhD diss., Utah State University); R. W. Ferrier, *The History of the British Petroleum Company, Vol. I: The Developing Years, 1901–1932* (Cambridge UP, 1982); Gabrielle Hecht, *Being Nuclear: Africans and the Global Uranium Market* (Duke UP, 2012); Suryakanthie Chetty, *'Africa Forms the Key': Alex Du Toit and the History of Continental Drift* (Springer, 2021); David Arnold, *Science, Technology and Medicine in Colonial India* (Cambridge UP, 2000); Roy MacLeod ed., *Nature and Empire: Science and the Colonial Encounter* (Manchester UP, 2000).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: the discipline's founding apparatus is metropolitan and industrial, and this is the row's principal limit — Lyell, Wernerian mineralogy and the Saxon/Harzburg/Freiberg *Bergwesen* mining-science tradition, with Felten himself anchoring "modern geology" in Central-European mining communities rather than overseas empire, and Mott T. Greene's *Geology in the Nineteenth Century* (Cornell UP, 1982) the standard overview. Colonial surveys therefore supplied a later parallel resource track, and I have explicitly not scored "colonisers used geologists to find ore" as formation. The strongest counter-argument to any high general score is that the twentieth-century transformation of the earth sciences (seismology, marine geology, palaeomagnetism, plate tectonics) is far better explained by military and Cold-War funding — Naomi Oreskes, *Science on a Mission* (U. of Chicago Press, 2021) — although Indian colonial seismology after the 1897 earthquake (Mallet/GSI) qualifies this rather than defeating it. British India (earliest national survey, 1851), Dutch (Bandung, oil and tin), French (BRGM, uranium), Portuguese (engineers before geologists, *Juntas de Propaganda*), German (Otavi/Tsumeb 1892–95) and US (public-lands survey, Alaska, Philippines, then Cold-War oceanography) are all distinct; the German and Portuguese cases rest on thinner verification. NA dimensions: D7 (agriculture/forestry/ecological transfer) — hydrogeology and engineering geology for irrigation are plausible but I found no dedicated verified source, so I have recorded insufficient evidence rather than a zero.

Confidence: high

### D09 — Sociology

Sociology's entanglement is real, well documented, and narrower than the "colonial origins" framing suggests, and it is concentrated in the period 1890s–1965 rather than in the whole canon. The mechanisms are state demand and funding (the Colonial Social Science Research Council 1944 and Colonial Development & Welfare research money steering agendas), an institutional field of overseas institutes and chairs (Rhodes-Livingstone/Manchester, SOAS, French overseas institutes), and classification as both object and method. France carries the strongest single-monograph case via Kabylie and Africanist sociology fused to the overseas empire; Britain's route runs through social anthropology, and Du Bois supplies the anti-colonial internal critique that the field also absorbed.

### D11 — Law

Law's entanglement is asymmetric in a way the row scores encode: D8 takes 3 because legal transplantation — law schools, the Inns of Court, code-making, courts, and the postwar "law and development" movement — is the mechanism by which the discipline became global, and a majority of the world's living law schools sit on transplanted colonial foundations. ### D12 — Economics

### D15 — History

### D16 — Demography / Population Studies

### D19 — Chemistry

Chemistry is the STEM discipline with the strongest genuine formation evidence, and D7 takes 3 on the plantation-complex: Kumar shows indigo production generating a sustained programme of applied chemistry — fermentation, adulteration testing, quality control, liaison with the German synthetic-dye industry — with planters, government departments and scientific advisers, and the Java cane-fertiliser case supplies the same pattern under Dutch rule. D4, D6 and D9 take 2 because colonial drug and raw-material specimens built the discipline's objects and standardisation practices: Crawford on chemical manipulation of cinchona bark in the Spanish Atlantic, Schiebinger on colonial bioprospecting, and the concrete archival chain running Imperial Institute → colonial products research → Tropical Products Institute (UK National Archives record series C1074).

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Prakash Kumar, *Indigo Plantations and Science in Colonial India* (Cambridge UP, 2005) and "Plantation science: improving natural indigo in colonial India, 1860–1913," *British Journal for the History of Science* (2007); Matthew James Crawford, "An Empire's Extract: Chemical Manipulations of Cinchona Bark in the Eighteenth-Century Spanish Atlantic World," *Osiris* (2014) and *The Andean Wonder Drug: Cinchona Bark and Imperial Science in the Spanish Atlantic, 1630–1800* (U. of Pittsburgh Press, 2016); Londa Schiebinger, *Plants and Empire: Colonial Bioprospecting in the Atlantic World* (Harvard UP, 2004); Sabine Clarke, "A Technocratic Imperial State? The Colonial Office and Scientific Research, 1940–1960," *Twentieth Century British History* 18(4) (2007), 453–480; "A Precocious Appetite: Industrial Agriculture and the Fertiliser Revolution in Java's Colonial Cane Fields, c. 1880–1914," *Journal of Southeast Asian Studies* 37 (2006); G. Roger Knight, *Commodities and Colonialism: The Story of 'Big Sugar' in Indonesia, 1880–1942* (KITLV, 2013); James Delbourgo & Nicholas Dew eds., *Science and Empire in the Atlantic World* (Routledge, 2008); *Survey of London*, vol. 38 (on the Imperial Institute, South Kensington).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: the theoretical and methodological core of academic chemistry is metropolitan, and the decisive disconfirming source is Anthony S. Travis, *The Rainbow Makers: The Origins of the Synthetic Dyestuffs Industry in Western Europe* (Lehigh UP/Associated UP, 1993), which shows synthetic-dye chemistry arising from German university–industry circuits whose relation to colonies was *substituting for* colonial raw materials — de-industrialising Indian indigo — an input/market and import-substituting relationship rather than a formation channel. That is why I have reserved the high scores for the applied, commodity, pharmaceutical and agricultural-chemistry complexes. Two further honest gaps: I found no verified study of mining assay laboratories or petrochemistry as colonial institutional channels for chemistry, so D5 is 2 rather than higher, and there is no verified monograph on colonial chemical-engineering education, so D8 is 2 rather than 3. The German-language monograph on Boehringer Mannheim's colonial entanglements (De Gruyter, reviewed in *Historische Zeitschrift* 2025) is confirmed as existing but I could not capture the author's name, and the French, US, Japanese and Egyptian chemistry routes returned nothing. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: medium

### D20 — Computer Science

Computer science scores near the floor, and the reason is not that information machinery lacked imperial reach but that the reach did not constitute the discipline. The best-documented channels are population classification (D3 = 2) and signals intelligence: punched-card systems were a global information regime spread through census bureaux, armies, railways and industry (Heide), calculative and tabulating technology entered Egypt through British occupation as an instrument of population control (Malak), identification regimes were built in the settler-colonial South African state and circulated globally (Breckenridge), and Mainwaring & Aldrich establish that British SIGINT retained an overseas and colonial-station network and a "colonial presence" after formal empire — the one channel where an imperial infrastructure plausibly fed equipment and career lines into British computing. Beyond that, Agar's *Government Machine* shows the computer descending from nation-state comptroller and statistical offices rather than colonial ones.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Lars Heide, *Punched-Card Systems and the Early Information Explosion, 1880–1945* (Johns Hopkins UP, 2009); Keith Breckenridge, *The Biometric State: The Global Politics of Identification and Surveillance in South Africa, 1850 to the Present* (Cambridge UP, 2014); Karim Malak, "Big Data Before the Age of Big Data: The 1917 Anglo-Egyptian 'Dress Rehearsal' of the Nazi Census," *Comparative Studies of South Asia, Africa and the Middle East* 45(3) (2025); Sarah Mainwaring & Richard J. Aldrich, "The Secret Empire of Signals Intelligence: GCHQ and the Persistence of the Colonial Presence," *International History Review* 43(1) (2021), 54–71; Thomas S. Mullaney, *The Chinese Typewriter: A History* (MIT Press, 2017) and *The Chinese Computer: A Global History of the Information Age* (MIT Press, 2024); Matthew H. Edney, *Mapping an Empire* (U. of Chicago Press, 1997), for the Great Trigonometrical Survey as an industrialised calculation regime.

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: chronology is decisive and I want it recorded. Electronic computing formed 1936–1962 (Turing's ACE/NPL, EDVAC, Manchester, EDSAC, the Moore School, then ARPA and the transistor/IC industry) and the departmental "computer science" wave came around 1962–1970, i.e. after classical colonialism, funded by defence and state R&D — Ceruzzi, *A History of Modern Computing*; Aspray, *John von Neumann and the Origins of Modern Computing*; Campbell-Kelly et al., *Computer: A History of the Information Machine*; Hodges, *Alan Turing*. Three specific mechanisms the research agenda raised could not be supported: I found **no** scholarly source tying Babbage's engines or his postal and statistical work to colonial administration as a formation mechanism; machine translation is Cold-War and strategic-linguistic, not missionary (Hutchins & Somers, *An Introduction to Machine Translation*, 1992); and the 1890 US census that drove punched-card tabulation is a national-administrative, not colonial, formation — of CTR/IBM's first non-US census customers only the Philippines is a colonial case. Colonial census bureaux did become permanent data infrastructures that postcolonial states inherited, which is why D3 is 2 and not 1, but attribution to colonialism specifically is partial because classification regimes were shared with metropolitan states. I also corrected a misattribution during research: *Programmed Inequality* is Marie Hicks (MIT Press, 2017) and concerns British state and gendered labour, not colonialism. Laidlow's *Empire and Information*, a *History of Computing in India*, and the "postcolonial histories of computing" think-piece all failed verification this pass. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: low

### D21 — Materials Science

Materials science gets a low but not a zero score, because colonial commodity research did create real institutions even though it did not create the academic discipline. The documented channels are rubber and strategic materials: the Rubber Research Institute of Malaya (*Nature*, 1937), Malaya's plantation economy as a scientifically managed colonial material system, gutta-percha and the telegraph (Tully), colonial-commodity collections organised through the Imperial Institute (1887, later the Commonwealth Institute) and the Tropical Products Institute (1958)→Natural Resources Institute line, and above all Clarke's account of a Colonial Office programme converting sugar and cane into synthetic raw materials and industrial feedstocks — the clearest statement I found of colonial demand shaping materials research. D4, D5 and D11 take 2 and the row sits at 0.91.

Strongest sources (for scores ≥2): Sabine Clarke, "A Technocratic Imperial State? The Colonial Office and Scientific Research, 1940–1960," *Twentieth Century British History* 18(4) (2007), 453–480; Sabine Clarke, *Science at the End of Empire: Experts and the Development of the British Caribbean, 1940–62* (Manchester UP, 2018), ch. 1 "New uses for sugar"; Lynn Hollen Lees, *Planting Empire, Cultivating Subjects: British Malaya, 1786–1941* (Cambridge UP, 2019), incl. "Rubber Reconstructs Malaya"; Fiona Williamson, "Rubber Wars: Rubber at the End of Empire in Colonial Malaya, c. 1945–57," *Journal of Contemporary History* 60(4) (2025), 607–624; John Tully, "A Victorian Ecological Disaster: Imperialism, the Telegraph, and Gutta-Percha," *Journal of World History* 20(4) (2009), 559–579; Jonathan Marshall, *To Have and Have Not: Southeast Asian Raw Materials and the Origins of the Pacific War* (U. of California Press, 1995); Stephen L. Harp ed., *A World History of Rubber: Empire, Industry, and the Everyday* (Wiley-Blackwell, 2015).

Uncertainty and heterogeneity: this is a case of documented institutions attaching to the wrong object. The colonial-side bodies were governmental and commodity research laboratories, not universities: they did not set the curriculum, the journals, the departmental structures or the canon of the field that became "materials science and engineering," whose formation is physics plus metallurgy plus ceramics under Air Force, ONR, ARPA and NSF money — Christoffer H. Gribbe & Kristofer Hallonsten, "The Emergence and Growth of Materials Science in Swedish Universities," *Historical Studies in the Natural Sciences* 47(4) (2017), 459–493, and Martin Mody's "Materials Science" entry in CMU's *ETHOS*. The naming and institutionalisation act (first MRLs, ARPA interdisciplinary laboratories) falls around 1953–1965, after classical colonialism. The strongest (b)-type claim available is that planter capital financed a metropolitan materials laboratory, since the British Rubber Producers' Research Association and its successor the Rubber and Plastics Research Association of Great Britain were plantation-funded (Science Museum Group party record) — but I found no historian's explicit claim that this formed the discipline, so I have not made it. Where claims say "empire shaped materials research" they often mean US informal empire and strategic-commodity access rather than formal colonial rule, which is a distinction this construct requires. I could not verify the Paley/Materials Policy Commission *Resources and Freedom* (1952), nor the specific "Colonial Products Research Council" as named, nor DSIR tropical-timbers or jute-research bodies, nor German *Werkstoffkunde*/Buna, Japanese Manchukuo resource research, or rare-earth histories. No dimensions scored NA.

Confidence: low
